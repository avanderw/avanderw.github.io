# The Wardley Doctrine

The Wardley Doctrine is a set of 40 universal rules for running an organization well. It's not about fancy strategy—it's about how to actually operate effectively. Think of it as the foundation that makes everything else possible. Without following these basic rules, organizations waste energy fighting themselves internally instead of competing externally.

## What Is Doctrine?

Doctrine is a set of about 40 universal principles—rules that work for any organization, in any industry, of any size. These aren't debatable. They're foundational requirements for being competitive.

The core ideas are simple: understand what your users actually need, know what you're doing, eliminate waste, and be transparent. These principles help organizations see clearly, act fast, and waste less energy on internal problems.   

## Doctrine vs. Strategy

**Doctrine** is the *how*—your operating manual. It's how you run day-to-day: communicate, make decisions, eliminate waste, learn from data.

**Strategy** (called "Gameplay" here) is the *what* and *where*—the specific moves you make based on your market situation.

Think of it this way: if your foundation (doctrine) is broken—people don't talk the same language, there's duplication everywhere—then even the best strategic plan will fail. Doctrine is the strength that lets you execute strategy fast and safely.   

## The Four Phases: Building Capability Step by Step

The 40 principles are meant to be implemented in order, not all at once. Each phase builds on the one before:

1. **Phase I: Stop Breaking Yourself** — Fix the obvious problems: unclear communication, wasted effort, bias.

2. **Phase II: Understand Your Situation** — Build the ability to see clearly and move fast, with teams that can make decisions on their own.

3. **Phase III: Do More with Less** — Optimize everything. Make great products cheaper and faster.

4. **Phase IV: Keep Evolving** — Make sure change is built into how you work, so you don't become obsolete.   

## Why Phases Matter

Trying to do everything at once doesn't work. If you try to "move fast" (Phase II) before you've fixed basic communication and eliminated waste (Phase I), you'll just move fast in the wrong direction. The phases build on each other. Each one makes the next one possible.

## The Six Areas Where Principles Apply

The 40 principles work across six parts of your organization:

- **Communication**: How you share information and use common language
- **Development**: How you build and improve things
- **Operations**: How you run day-to-day work
- **Learning**: How you get smarter from data and feedback
- **Leadership**: How leaders make decisions and inspire people
- **Structure**: How you organize teams and distribute power

No area is ignored. The doctrine touches every part of the organization.

## A Key Insight: Organization Design Is Strategy

Look at the principles across all phases, and you'll notice something: most of them are about how you organize and structure the company. This tells you something important: **how you organize is your strategy**.

Instead of worrying just about markets and competitors, the doctrine says: fix your internal organization first. Small teams. Clear roles. People who feel ownership. Transparency. These structural choices matter more than most strategies because they determine whether you can actually execute anything.

## Phase I: Stop Breaking Yourself

Phase I is about fixing the foundation. You can't build anything stable on a broken base. These principles eliminate the ways organizations sabotage themselves.

### Make Communication Clear

**Principle 1: Use a common language** — When marketing says one thing and engineering says another, nothing works. Everyone must use the same diagrams and terms to describe what you're building. If you can't map or explain it clearly, stop and don't proceed until you can.

**Principle 2: Challenge assumptions** — Question things. Just because we've always done it this way doesn't mean it still works.

**Principle 3: Understand your situation** — Before making decisions, know what you're dealing with: what are your components, what are your dependencies, what's changing?

### Build for Value

**Principles 4 & 5: Understand your users and build what they actually need** — Know your customers, employees, investors, and regulators. Don't build for internal politics or pet projects—build for real needs.

**Principle 6: Remove waste and duplication** — Stop doing the same work twice. Stop guessing when you can use data. Eliminate waste.

**Principle 7: Use the right approach for the job** — A new feature needs a different approach than a mature product does. Choose your method based on what you're actually building.

### Learn from Data and Keep Details in Mind

**Principle 8: Use data to learn** — Prefer measurable feedback over opinions. Make decisions based on evidence.

**Principle 9: Know the details** — Leaders must actually understand what's happening on the ground. Don't abstract away operational reality. Know your components and their dependencies.

### Why Phase I Matters

All this seems basic—and it is. But most organizations skip it. They don't speak the same language. They do work multiple times. They make decisions without data. This wastes enormous amounts of energy. Phase I simply removes that waste so energy goes toward real work instead of internal fighting.

## Phase II: Understand Your Situation and Move Fast

Now that the foundation is solid, you can build speed and distribute decision-making. You have clarity (Phase I), so you can afford to move quickly.   

### Leadership That Values Speed

**Principle 16: Move fast** — An imperfect plan executed today beats a perfect plan executed next month. Act now and adjust.

**Principle 17: Strategy evolves** — Don't lock into a rigid long-term plan. Strategy is a cycle of trying, learning, and adjusting.

**Principle 18: Overcome resistance** — You'll face inertia: people invested in the old way, political obstacles, past decisions that feel permanent. Name it and push through.

### Development That's Practical

**Principles 11 & 12: Focus on outcomes, be pragmatic** — Results matter. Whether you follow process perfectly doesn't. If it works, it works.

**Principle 14: Think FIRE** — Fast, Inexpensive, Restrained, Elegant. Build lean. Mistakes should be cheap to fix. Make solutions simple.

**Principle 13: Use good tools** — Use visualization tools like maps to understand your landscape. Use financial tools to ground decisions in reality.

**Principle 15: Standardize when it makes sense** — Mature, stable parts should be standardized. Novel parts should be free to experiment.

### Work Smart

**Principle 21: Effectiveness before efficiency** — First, make sure you're doing the right thing. Only after that, optimize how you do it. Doing the wrong thing fast is just waste.

**Principle 20: Plan for failure** — When you move fast, some things will fail. Design so failures stay small and contained.

**Principle 19: Learn by doing** — Get real feedback from actual work, not just theories and plans.

**Principle 10: Share information openly** — Information should flow freely to everyone who needs it. This lets teams make good decisions without waiting for approval.

### Organize for Speed

**Principle 23: Small teams** — Use small teams (the old "two pizza teams" concept—small enough to feed with two pizzas). Smaller teams communicate better and move faster.

**Principle 24: Distribute decision-making** — Don't make teams wait for central approval. Push decisions down to the teams closest to the work. Let them decide.

**Principle 22: Hire the right people** — When teams have autonomy, capability and attitude matter more than titles or experience. Hire for ability to learn and ownership mindset.

### The Speed/Safety Balance

This phase seems contradictory: go fast, but manage failures? That works because you've built transparency, small teams, and shared understanding from Phase I. Now distributed teams can move quickly without fragmenting because they share language and see the same map.   

## Phase III: Do More with Less

You're now fast and coordinated. Phase III is about converting that speed into cost advantage. Optimize relentlessly. Raise your standards.

### Leadership: Own It and Think Big

**Principle 25: Own your outcomes** — Full personal responsibility. No excuses. No blame-shifting.

**Principle 26: Think big** — Set ambitious direction. Inspire people. Give them something worth working toward.

**Principle 28: Commit to direction, adjust tactics** — Know where you're going, but be flexible about the path. It's like crossing a river by feeling the stones—keep moving toward the other side but adjust your steps as you learn.

**Principles 27 & 29: Strategy is hard, be humble** — The future is uncertain and complex. Listen more. Be willing to admit what you don't know.

### Operations: Remove Bottlenecks

**Principle 31: Optimize flow** — Find what's slowing you down—political, technical, structural—and remove it. Ruthlessly. Anything blocking value delivery is your enemy.

**Principle 32: Do better with less** — Same output with fewer resources. Better output with same resources. Constantly.

**Principle 33: Set exceptional standards** — Stop accepting "good enough." Great becomes the minimum.

### People: Hire the Best, Keep Them Engaged

**Principle 34: Hire exceptional people** — In a fast, autonomous culture, this matters more than ever.

**Principle 35: Give people purpose, mastery, and autonomy** — People work hardest when they understand why their work matters, can see themselves getting better, and have control over how they do it.

**Principle 30: Embrace new ideas** — Curiosity and smart risk-taking keep you from becoming stale.

### The Economic Converter

Flow optimization is the key. When you remove blockages, work moves faster. Feedback comes sooner. Mistakes get caught earlier. You do more with the same resources. That's how speed becomes cost advantage.

## Phase IV: Keep Evolving

You're optimized. You're efficient. Now what? Phase IV is about making sure you don't become obsolete. Everything changes. Your job is to build an organization that changes with it.

### Structure: Change Is Built In

**Principle 39: Design the organization for change** — Don't build something rigid that resists change. Build something that expects it and adapts to it.

**Principle 40: There is no one culture** — Different parts of your organization do different things. You need Pioneers (exploring new ideas), Settlers (scaling those ideas), and Town Planners (optimizing and industrializing). Manage the tension between them. They're all necessary.

### Leadership and Learning: The Future Matters More Than Today

**Principle 37: Everything is temporary** — Even your best product or core competency will eventually become a commodity or obsolete. Accept that. It changes how you plan.

**Principle 36: Use your understanding to win** — You now have maps, you understand your landscape. Use that knowledge strategically.

**Principle 38: Listen to your ecosystem** — Customers, partners, open-source communities—they signal the future. Pay attention to external signals. They're your early warning system.

### Why Phase IV Matters

Without Phase IV, even the best, most optimized organizations eventually fail. They stop learning. They assume yesterday's success will continue. They don't see the disruption coming. Phase IV embeds renewal into how you work. It makes adaptation automatic, not something you have to remember to do.

## How It All Works Together

**Doctrine enables strategy.** You can't execute great strategy on a broken foundation. The 40 principles give you that solid foundation—clear communication, no waste, fast decision-making, autonomy.

**Strategy without doctrine fails.** Even a brilliant strategic move (Gameplay) will fail if the organization can't execute it. You can't move fast if people don't speak the same language. You can't compete on cost if there's duplication everywhere.

**Culture matters.** Different parts of your organization need different mindsets. Pioneers explore. Settlers scale. Town Planners optimize. That's not a bug—it's necessary. The doctrine acknowledges this and tells you how to manage it.
