# The Paradox of Agency at Work

Many companies say they want people who take ownership. They hire people who can make decisions, challenge assumptions, and keep moving when the answer is not obvious.

Then the new hire arrives and discovers that every decision needs approval, every disagreement is treated as disloyalty, and every experiment must be planned so carefully that it is no longer an experiment.

The company wanted initiative. Its management system wanted obedience.

That is the paradox of agency at work: organizations recruit independent thinkers, then slowly train them to wait.

## The gap between hiring and managing

During hiring, autonomy sounds attractive. A candidate who says, "I would try this," is more appealing than one who says, "I would wait for instructions." Leaders want people who can handle ambiguity and solve problems without constant supervision.

The difficulty starts after the offer is accepted. Independent people bring independent judgment. They ask why a process exists. They point out when a plan no longer fits the facts. They make a reasonable decision without asking permission for every small step.

Those behaviours are useful when the team is trying to learn. They are uncomfortable when a manager equates leadership with being the person who has the answer.

A manager can hire an excellent person for three reasons:

- to solve difficult problems;
- to make the team smarter; or
- to signal that the manager can attract excellent people.

Only the first two reasons create value. In the third case, the talented employee becomes a trophy: impressive to have, but not trusted with meaningful work.

## What suppression looks like

Suppression is rarely announced as, "Stop thinking." It usually arrives as a series of reasonable-sounding decisions:

- "Keep me informed" becomes approval for every detail.
- "We need alignment" becomes agreement before discussion.
- "Follow the process" becomes a reason not to improve the process.
- "Be a team player" becomes a request not to challenge the plan.
- "We tried that before" becomes a permanent veto.

None of these phrases is always wrong. The problem is what happens when they are used to protect a manager's control rather than the team's outcome.

The result is predictable. The employee stops volunteering ideas because ideas create work and risk without giving them any influence. They still complete assigned tasks, but they stop bringing their best judgment to the job. The organization keeps the person's labour and loses their intelligence.

## Why good managers do this

The explanation does not need to be a diagnosis of the manager's personality. Often, several ordinary pressures are enough:

1. **Control feels safer than uncertainty.** A manager under pressure may prefer a visible plan to a useful experiment, even when the plan is based on weak assumptions.
2. **Disagreement feels personal.** When a manager's status depends on being the expert, a challenge to an idea can feel like a challenge to their authority.
3. **Metrics reward predictability.** Quarterly targets and audit requirements can make a thoughtful, uncertain path look worse than a confident but ineffective one.
4. **The manager lacks practice with conflict.** It is easier to shut down a difficult conversation than to keep it productive.

This is how a team can promise autonomy while operating through compliance. The stated values are commitment, ownership, and learning. The daily signals are caution, permission, and conformity.

## Healthy autonomy is not anarchy

Autonomy does not mean that everyone can do whatever they want. Good teams still need boundaries. They need clear goals, decision rights, constraints, and ways to make risks visible.

The important distinction is between a boundary and a bottleneck.

> A boundary explains what must not happen. A bottleneck requires one person to approve everything.

A strong manager says, "This is the outcome we need, these constraints matter, and you own the approach. Bring me the trade-offs." A controlling manager says, "Here is the approach. Ask before changing anything."

The first model creates accountability. The second creates dependence.

## The cost of turning people into spectators

When capable people are repeatedly overruled, they adapt. They ask fewer questions. They stop raising uncomfortable information. They do exactly what was requested and no more.

This is often described as disengagement or quiet quitting, but the simpler description is cognitive withdrawal: the person is present, but their attention is no longer invested in improving the work.

The cost is larger than morale. Teams lose:

- early warnings about risks;
- alternative solutions;
- faster decisions close to the work;
- learning from small experiments; and
- the motivation to take responsibility for outcomes.

Over time, the manager becomes even more central because nobody else is encouraged to decide. That increased dependence then appears to confirm the manager's belief that the team cannot operate without them.

## A practical test for leaders

Managers do not need a new leadership theory to check whether they are suppressing agency. They can look at the work itself.

Ask:

- What decisions can this person make without me?
- When did someone last change my mind?
- Do I ask for dissent before making a decision, or only after something goes wrong?
- Are our processes protecting a real constraint or preserving an old habit?
- Does my feedback improve the person's judgment, or merely make them more cautious?
- If I were away for two weeks, would the team have clarity or just a backlog of approvals?

The answers are more useful than a leadership label. A manager who wants more initiative must give people a real problem, enough context to make decisions, and room to be wrong in small, recoverable ways.

## A better operating agreement

Teams can make autonomy explicit instead of leaving it to interpretation. For each area of work, agree on:

- the outcome that matters;
- the decisions the owner can make alone;
- the decisions that require consultation;
- the constraints that cannot be crossed;
- the evidence that should trigger a change of direction; and
- how and when progress will be reported.

This makes disagreement safer because the conversation is about the work, not the manager's authority. It also makes accountability fairer: people cannot be blamed for failing to own decisions they were never allowed to make.

## The real leadership test

Hiring autonomous people is easy to praise. Managing them well is harder because it requires the leader to give up some control without giving up responsibility.

The test is not whether a manager can attract strong people. It is whether strong people become more capable, more candid, and more useful after joining the team.

If a company wants initiative, it must make initiative safe. If it wants challenge, it must make disagreement useful. If it wants ownership, it must stop requiring permission for every meaningful decision.

Otherwise, the organization is not building a team of high-agency people. It is collecting them, then teaching them to wait.
