# Getting Things Done

I read *Getting Things Done* a while back, and it took a few years of trial and error to land on a version of the method I'd actually keep using. The core diagnosis is still the reason I bother: most of my stress wasn't coming from having too much to do — it was coming from having too much *undecided*. Half-formed commitments sitting in my head, in old emails, in notes I'd never look at again. David Allen calls these "open loops." The book is a system for closing them.

Here's the version that's actually running.

## Capture

I'd love a single inbox. In practice it doesn't hold up: a synchronous call, an async chat thread, an email that sits for two days, and a voice note I record while driving all arrive on completely different rhythms. Trying to force them into one landing spot just adds a manual step between "thing happens" and "thing captured" — and that step is exactly where capture dies. So I let each channel be its own inbox — email, chat, tasks, notes, voice commands in the car — and instead standardize the process each one runs through, not the location.

The one split I do enforce: communication (email, chat) is a landing area, not a home. From there, each thing either becomes a task — if it's a quick, well-defined win — or a note, if what I actually need is to retain the information rather than act on it. Tasks get worked and closed. Notes are the only thing I actively garden — periodically revisited and cleaned up, because their job is to build a memory over time, not get done.

## Clarify

Whatever lands in an inbox doesn't get to just sit there. Is it actionable? If not, it's trash, reference material for the notes, or someday/maybe. If it is actionable, the only question that matters is: what's the next physical action?

## Next actions

Almost every stalled task I have isn't stalled because it's hard — it's stalled because it's vague. "Plan the offsite" isn't a task, it's a fog. Nobody can *do* a fog. But "email Sarah to ask which week works" — that's a task, and it fits in one sitting.

This is close to my own first-milestone thinking: the way to actually start something isn't to look at the whole outcome, it's to define the smallest concrete step that counts as progress. [See the first milestone principle](/principles). GTD's next action is that same idea applied to every single task on the list — a verb, an object, one sitting. Most of my procrastination turned out not to be laziness. It was that I'd written down outcomes instead of actions.

## One list

In Google Tasks I run five lists: **Inbox**, **Next**, **Waiting**, **Someday/Maybe**, and **Recurring**. Inbox is capture; everything else is where things land once they're clarified — Next for anything with a defined next action, Waiting for things I've delegated or am blocked on, Someday/Maybe for things I'm not committing to yet.

Work and personal live in the same lists on purpose. I've tried splitting them, and the problem isn't cleanliness — it's blind spots. If personal tasks are invisible while I'm "in work mode," I stop noticing the ones that can only be actioned during work hours: a call to a business that closes at 5, an errand on the way to the office. Keeping everything in one view means I actually see those windows instead of missing them because I'd mentally filed the task under the wrong hat.

No folder or list per project, either — I want Next to be one view I can scan in ten seconds. Google Tasks doesn't have real tags, so "project" lives as a prefix in the title or a line in the description — enough to search when I need that view, without splitting the list I actually work from.

## Recurring tasks

Recurring holds the stuff that needs to exist for its own sake — system automations, reminders, habits — and I keep it off the calendar on purpose. My calendar is for things with a real time cost that I'd actually protect; the moment it fills up with recurring nags, I stop trusting it and start tuning it out — it goes tone deaf. Google Tasks can still lift recurring items into the calendar view when I want to see everything in one place, so I'm not losing visibility, just keeping the calendar itself strict.

## Voice capture

Voice capture in the car already means I don't lose a thought just because my hands are on the wheel. The next step I'm considering is a Discord or WhatsApp bot I can just talk to, which auto-categorizes what I send it — this is very much in line with where AI agents are heading in 2026: systems that take unstructured voice, text, or chat input and route it into the right list or note without me doing the sorting by hand. If that lands, capture stops being "get it into an inbox" and starts being "say it to the bot, trust the categorization."

## Daily practice

Capture wherever I am — including by voice. Split communication into task-or-note. Force every task into a real next action. Keep one flat list across work and personal instead of per-project folders. Keep recurring off the calendar so the calendar stays trustworthy. Let notes be the only thing I slow down to garden.
